Understanding Cartilage Damage: Causes, Symptoms, and Treatment Options

Key Takeaway: Articular cartilage cannot heal on its own because it lacks blood supply. Modern cartilage repair techniques — including microfracture, OATS, MACI, and fresh cartilage transplantation — can restore joint surface integrity and delay or prevent the need for joint replacement in younger, active patients.

What Is Articular Cartilage and Why Is It Important?

Articular cartilage is the smooth, white tissue that covers the ends of bones where they meet to form joints. It serves two critical functions: providing a nearly frictionless surface for joint movement (the coefficient of friction is lower than ice on ice) and distributing loads across the joint surface to protect the underlying bone.

Healthy cartilage is 2-4mm thick in most joints and is composed primarily of water (65-80%), type II collagen fibers, and proteoglycans. Unlike most tissues in the body, articular cartilage has no blood vessels, no nerves, and no lymphatic drainage. This means it relies entirely on synovial fluid (joint fluid) for nutrition — and it has extremely limited capacity to repair itself when damaged.

What Causes Cartilage Damage?

Acute trauma: A sudden impact, twist, or fall can damage cartilage directly. Sports injuries — particularly those involving pivoting (soccer, basketball, skiing) — are common causes. ACL tears are frequently associated with concurrent cartilage damage.

Repetitive wear: Chronic overloading of a joint — from years of high-impact activity, obesity, or malalignment — gradually breaks down cartilage. This is the mechanism behind osteoarthritis, the most common form of arthritis.

Osteochondritis dissecans (OCD): A condition where a segment of bone and overlying cartilage loses blood supply and can separate from the surrounding joint surface. Most common in adolescents and young adults.

Joint malalignment: When the mechanical axis of the leg is shifted (bowlegged or knock-kneed alignment), load is concentrated on one side of the joint, accelerating cartilage wear in that compartment.

How Is Cartilage Damage Classified?

The International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) grades cartilage damage on a scale of 0-4:

Grade 0: Normal, healthy cartilage. Grade 1: Softening or superficial blistering of the cartilage surface. Grade 2: Partial-thickness defect — less than 50% of cartilage depth is affected. Grade 3: Deep defect — more than 50% of cartilage depth, with fissuring down to but not through the subchondral bone. Grade 4: Full-thickness defect — cartilage is completely worn away, exposing the underlying bone.

What Are the Symptoms of Cartilage Damage?

Cartilage itself has no nerve endings, so damage to cartilage alone may not cause pain initially. Symptoms typically develop as the condition progresses: pain with activity (especially impact or twisting movements), swelling (the joint produces excess fluid in response to the damage), catching or locking (loose cartilage fragments can interfere with joint motion), grinding or crepitus (rough surfaces rub against each other), and stiffness after periods of inactivity.

How Is Cartilage Damage Diagnosed?

MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) is the gold standard for non-invasive cartilage evaluation. Modern 3T MRI scanners with cartilage-specific sequences can detect cartilage defects, measure cartilage thickness, and assess the underlying bone. X-rays can show joint space narrowing (an indirect sign of cartilage loss) and bone changes but cannot directly visualize cartilage. Diagnostic arthroscopy provides the most detailed evaluation — the surgeon can directly probe the cartilage to assess its firmness, stability, and the exact size and depth of any defects.

What Are the Treatment Options for Cartilage Damage?

Non-Surgical Options

Physical therapy: Strengthening the muscles around the joint reduces load on the cartilage. Activity modification: Switching from high-impact to low-impact activities. Anti-inflammatory medications: NSAIDs can reduce pain and swelling. Injections: Hyaluronic acid (viscosupplementation) or PRP therapy can provide symptomatic relief and may slow cartilage degeneration. Bracing: Unloader braces can shift weight away from the damaged compartment.

Microfracture

The surgeon uses a small awl to create tiny holes (microfractures) in the bone beneath the cartilage defect. This allows bone marrow stem cells to migrate into the defect and form a clot that matures into fibrocartilage — a repair tissue that is functional but not as durable as native hyaline cartilage. Best for small defects (under 2 cm²) in younger patients.

OATS (Osteochondral Autograft Transfer)

Also called mosaicplasty, this procedure transfers one or more small plugs of healthy cartilage and bone from a non-weight-bearing area of the knee to fill the defect. The transplanted plugs provide native hyaline cartilage coverage. Best for medium-sized defects (1-4 cm²).

MACI (Matrix-Induced Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation)

A two-stage procedure. First, a small sample of healthy cartilage is harvested arthroscopically and sent to a lab where the cartilage cells (chondrocytes) are cultured and expanded on a collagen membrane. In the second surgery, this cell-seeded membrane is implanted into the defect. MACI is FDA-approved and suitable for larger defects (up to 20 cm²).

Fresh Osteochondral Allograft (OCA)

For the largest defects, a size-matched segment of cartilage and bone from a fresh donor is transplanted into the patient's joint. This provides mature, hyaline cartilage from day one. Fresh allografts must be implanted within 28 days of harvest to maintain cell viability. Best for large defects or failed previous repairs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can damaged cartilage heal on its own?

Articular cartilage has very limited self-healing ability because it lacks blood supply. Small defects may remain stable, but larger or deeper lesions typically progress without treatment. Early intervention is key to preserving the joint.

What is the difference between microfracture and cartilage transplant?

Microfracture stimulates the body to produce fibrocartilage (scar cartilage) by creating tiny holes in bone. Cartilage transplant (OATS, OCA) moves actual hyaline cartilage into the defect for more durable, native-quality repair. The choice depends on defect size and patient factors.

How long is recovery from cartilage repair surgery?

Microfracture: 4-6 months. OATS: 4-6 months. MACI/ACI: 9-12 months. All require protected weight-bearing and structured rehab to allow the repair to mature.

Who is a good candidate for cartilage repair?

Best candidates are active patients under 50 with isolated cartilage defects, healthy surrounding cartilage, stable ligaments, and proper joint alignment. Those with widespread arthritis may need different approaches.
