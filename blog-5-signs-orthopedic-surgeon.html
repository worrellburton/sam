Orthopedic Health

5 Signs You Need to See an Orthopedic Surgeon

March 10, 2026 • 8 min read • By Dr. Sameh Elguizaoui, M.D.

Key Takeaways

  • Joint pain lasting more than two weeks warrants professional evaluation
  • Limited range of motion and chronic swelling are signs of underlying damage
  • Feelings of joint instability may indicate ligament injury
  • Pain that disrupts sleep or daily activities should not be ignored
  • Early intervention often leads to better outcomes and less invasive treatment

When Pain Becomes a Warning Sign

Most of us have experienced minor aches and pains after a tough workout or a long day on our feet. These typically resolve within a few days with rest and over-the-counter medication. But what happens when the pain doesn't go away? When stiffness becomes your morning routine, or when a joint that used to move freely now catches, clicks, or gives out?

These are the moments that mark the beginning of a journey — one that millions of Americans take each year when they realize their body is telling them something important. Ignoring these signals can lead to worsening damage, longer recovery times, and more invasive treatments down the road. Here are five signs that it's time to see an orthopedic specialist.

Sign 1: Persistent Joint Pain That Won't Resolve

Occasional joint pain is normal, especially after physical activity. However, pain that persists for more than two weeks — despite rest, ice, and over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medications — is a red flag that something deeper may be going on.

Persistent pain can indicate conditions such as osteoarthritis, tendinitis, bursitis, or stress fractures. The character of the pain matters too: a dull, constant ache suggests different pathology than sharp, stabbing pain with specific movements. An orthopedic surgeon can perform a thorough examination and order appropriate imaging to identify the source.

Sign 2: Limited Range of Motion

If you can no longer fully bend your knee, raise your arm overhead, or rotate your shoulder without difficulty, you may have a structural problem that needs attention. Limited range of motion often develops gradually — you might not notice it until you can't reach a high shelf or struggle to tie your shoes.

Common causes include frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis), meniscus tears, rotator cuff injuries, and advanced arthritis. Physical therapy can help in many cases, but a proper diagnosis is essential to determine the right treatment approach. Early intervention with joint preservation techniques can prevent the need for more aggressive procedures later.

Sign 3: Swelling That Keeps Coming Back

Acute swelling after an injury is your body's natural inflammatory response. But when swelling becomes chronic — recurring regularly or never fully subsiding — it often signals ongoing damage within the joint. This could be a sign of a cartilage injury, synovitis (inflammation of the joint lining), or an autoimmune condition affecting the joints.

Joint effusion, the medical term for excess fluid in a joint, can cause stiffness, warmth, and visible puffiness around the knee, ankle, or other joints. If you notice that your joint repeatedly swells after normal activities, it's time for a professional evaluation.

Sign 4: Instability or "Giving Way" Sensations

If your knee buckles when you walk down stairs, or your ankle rolls unexpectedly on flat ground, you may have ligament damage that compromises joint stability. The sensation of a joint "giving way" is one of the most common symptoms of an ACL tear, though it can also indicate other ligament injuries or patellar instability.

Joint instability isn't just uncomfortable — it's dangerous. Each episode of giving way can cause additional damage to cartilage and other structures within the joint. Surgical stabilization may be necessary to prevent progressive joint deterioration, especially in active individuals who want to return to sports.

Sign 5: Pain That Disrupts Sleep or Daily Activities

When orthopedic pain begins affecting your quality of life — keeping you awake at night, preventing you from exercising, making it difficult to climb stairs or carry groceries — it has crossed a threshold that warrants specialist attention. Night pain, in particular, can be a sign of more serious conditions including bone tumors (rare but important to rule out), advanced arthritis, or rotator cuff tears.

The impact on daily function is often the tipping point that brings patients to our office. You shouldn't have to redesign your life around pain. Modern orthopedic medicine offers a wide range of solutions, from regenerative therapies to minimally invasive surgery, that can restore your function and comfort.

What to Expect at Your First Orthopedic Visit

Your first appointment will typically include a detailed medical history, physical examination of the affected joint, and possibly imaging studies such as X-rays or an MRI. Dr. Elguizaoui will discuss your symptoms, activity level, and goals to develop a personalized treatment plan.

Many conditions can be managed with conservative treatments including physical therapy, anti-inflammatory medications, corticosteroid injections, or PRP therapy. Surgery is typically recommended only when these approaches haven't provided adequate relief.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I see an orthopedic surgeon vs. my primary care doctor?

See an orthopedic surgeon when pain persists beyond 2 weeks despite home treatment, you have limited range of motion, recurring swelling, joint instability, or when your primary care doctor's treatments aren't providing relief. An orthopedic specialist has advanced training in musculoskeletal conditions and can offer targeted diagnostic and treatment options.

Do I need a referral to see an orthopedic surgeon?

Most orthopedic surgeons, including Dr. Elguizaoui, accept self-referrals. However, some insurance plans (particularly HMOs) may require a referral from your primary care physician. Check with your insurance provider before scheduling.

What should I bring to my first orthopedic appointment?

Bring your insurance card, photo ID, a list of current medications, any prior imaging studies (X-rays, MRIs) on CD or uploaded to a patient portal, and a written list of your symptoms and questions. Wear comfortable clothing that allows easy access to the affected joint.

Will I need surgery?

Not necessarily. Many orthopedic conditions respond well to conservative treatments such as physical therapy, injections, bracing, or activity modification. Surgery is typically considered only after conservative approaches have been given adequate time to work.

How quickly can I get an appointment?
