PRP Therapy in Sports Medicine: Does It Really Work?

Key Takeaway: PRP (platelet-rich plasma) therapy uses concentrated growth factors from your own blood to accelerate healing of tendons, ligaments, and mild cartilage damage. Research shows the strongest evidence for chronic tendon injuries like tennis elbow, patellar tendinitis, and Achilles tendinopathy.

What Is PRP Therapy?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy is a regenerative medicine treatment that harnesses your body's own healing mechanisms. A small amount of blood is drawn from your arm, processed in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets to 3-5 times normal levels, and then injected into the injured tissue under ultrasound guidance.

Platelets are best known for their role in blood clotting, but they also contain over 800 proteins called growth factors that are critical for tissue repair. By concentrating these growth factors and delivering them directly to an injury site, PRP therapy aims to jumpstart and accelerate the body's natural healing process.

"PRP isn't a magic cure-all, but for the right conditions and the right patients, it can be remarkably effective. I've seen it help patients avoid surgery for chronic tendon injuries that hadn't responded to months of physical therapy and cortisone injections."

How Does PRP Therapy Work at the Cellular Level?

When platelets are activated at an injury site, they release a cascade of growth factors including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1). These growth factors work together to:

Recruit stem cells to the injury site, providing raw material for tissue regeneration. Stimulate collagen production, rebuilding the structural framework of tendons and ligaments. Promote new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis), improving blood supply to poorly vascularized tissues. Reduce inflammation by modulating the immune response, shifting from destructive to reparative processes.

What Conditions Does PRP Therapy Treat?

PRP therapy has shown the most consistent clinical evidence for:

Chronic tendon injuries (tendinopathy): Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis), golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis), patellar tendinitis (jumper's knee), Achilles tendinopathy, and rotator cuff tendinitis. Multiple randomized controlled trials have shown PRP to be superior to cortisone injections for these conditions, with more durable results.

Mild to moderate osteoarthritis: PRP injections into arthritic joints — particularly the knee — have shown promise in reducing pain and improving function for patients with early-stage arthritis. The evidence is strongest for mild to moderate arthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grades 1-3).

Muscle injuries: Some evidence supports PRP for accelerating healing of acute muscle tears, particularly hamstring strains. Several professional sports teams use PRP as part of their treatment protocols for muscle injuries.

Ligament injuries: PRP may help partial ligament tears heal more effectively when combined with rehabilitation, potentially avoiding the need for surgical reconstruction in select cases.

What Does the Research Say?

The evidence for PRP therapy has matured significantly over the past decade. A 2021 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Sports Medicine found that PRP was significantly more effective than cortisone injections for lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) at 6-month and 12-month follow-up, with sustained improvement compared to the temporary relief of cortisone.

For knee osteoarthritis, a 2020 systematic review in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery found that PRP injections provided greater pain relief and functional improvement compared to hyaluronic acid injections at 12-month follow-up.

It's important to note that PRP preparation methods vary between clinics, which can affect outcomes. The concentration of platelets, whether white blood cells are included (leukocyte-rich vs. leukocyte-poor PRP), and the activation method all influence the biological activity of the final product.

What Should You Expect During PRP Treatment?

The PRP procedure is performed in-office and typically takes about 30 minutes. A blood sample (usually 30-60mL) is drawn from your arm, similar to a routine blood test. The blood is placed in a centrifuge that separates it into layers. The platelet-rich layer is extracted, and the concentrated PRP is injected into the injury site using ultrasound guidance for precision.

After the injection, you may experience mild soreness at the injection site for 2-3 days. You should avoid anti-inflammatory medications (ibuprofen, naproxen) for 1-2 weeks, as these can interfere with the inflammatory healing cascade that PRP initiates. Light activity is encouraged, with gradual return to full activity over 2-6 weeks depending on the condition treated.

Who Is a Good Candidate for PRP Therapy?

PRP therapy works best for patients who have chronic tendon or ligament injuries that haven't responded to physical therapy and rest, want to avoid or delay surgery, have mild to moderate osteoarthritis, and are willing to invest time in the healing process (PRP works gradually over weeks, not instantly). Patients who are not good candidates include those with active infection, blood disorders, cancer, or those taking blood thinners.

PRP vs. Cortisone: While cortisone injections provide faster initial pain relief, PRP therapy provides more durable, long-lasting results. Cortisone is anti-inflammatory but doesn't promote healing — and repeated cortisone injections can actually weaken tendons. PRP promotes actual tissue repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does PRP therapy work?

PRP concentrates platelets from your own blood to 3-5 times normal levels. When injected into injured tissue, the platelets release growth factors that stimulate tissue repair, reduce inflammation, and promote healing of tendons, ligaments, and cartilage.

Is PRP therapy painful?

The blood draw is similar to a routine lab test. The injection may cause temporary discomfort, similar to a cortisone injection. Some patients experience mild soreness for 2-3 days afterward. Local anesthesia and ultrasound guidance are used to minimize discomfort.

How many PRP treatments do I need?

Most protocols involve 1-3 injections spaced 2-4 weeks apart. Some patients improve significantly after a single injection, while chronic conditions may require a full series.

Does insurance cover PRP therapy?

Most insurance plans do not currently cover PRP therapy. However, coverage is expanding as clinical evidence grows. Check with your insurance provider for your specific plan details.
